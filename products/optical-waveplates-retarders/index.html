Optical Waveplates & Retarders

Material Options

Select the optimal optical material based on your wavelength, power, thermal environment and mechanical requirements.

  • Crystalline Quartz (0-degree) — Standard waveplate material for visible-NIR. Excellent optical quality and availability.
  • Crystalline Quartz (5-degree Y-cut) — For UV applications. Reduced chromatic dispersion compared to 0-degree cut.
  • MgF2 — Birefringent crystal for deep UV waveplates. Transmits down to 115nm. Lower birefringence than quartz.

Coating Options

Coatings are engineered around your specific wavelength and operating conditions.

  • BBAR VIS-NIR — R < 0.5% across 400-1000nm. Standard for multi-wavelength visible-NIR systems.
  • AR @ 532nm — R < 0.1% per surface. For green laser polarization control.
  • AR @ 1064nm — R < 0.1% per surface. For Nd:YAG laser systems.
  • UV AR (< 400nm) — R < 0.5% for UV laser and spectroscopy systems.

Engineering Considerations

Zero-order vs multi-order: Zero-order waveplates (single plate or compound) have retardance accuracy that is largely independent of wavelength changes. Multi-order waveplates are less expensive but highly wavelength-sensitive — a 1nm wavelength shift can change retardance significantly. Use zero-order for precision applications.

Achromatic waveplates: For broadband or tunable wavelength systems, achromatic waveplates (compound quartz+MgF2) maintain near-constant retardance across a wide wavelength range. Essential for Ti:Sapphire, OPO and supercontinuum systems.

Temperature sensitivity: Quartz waveplate retardance changes with temperature (~0.01 waves/degree C for zero-order). For systems with large temperature swings, specify temperature-compensated designs or allow for retarding adjustment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between zero-order and multi-order waveplates?
Zero-order waveplates have retardance accuracy largely independent of wavelength, making them suitable for precision and broadband applications. Multi-order waveplates are thinner, less expensive, but highly wavelength-sensitive. Use zero-order when retardance accuracy is critical.
Can I use a half-wave plate to convert linear to circular polarization?
No. A quarter-wave plate with its fast axis at 45 degrees to the linear polarization direction converts linear to circular polarization. A half-wave plate rotates the polarization direction but maintains linear polarization.
