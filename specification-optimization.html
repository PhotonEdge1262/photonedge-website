Engineering Guide

Optical Specification Optimization

Every engineer wants the best possible component. But "best" and "most expensive" are not the same thing. Tighter tolerances mean higher cost — but they don't always deliver better system performance.

The goal is not the tightest spec. It's the right spec for the job.

Surface Flatness: λ/4 vs λ/10 vs λ/20

Surface flatness describes how much an optical surface deviates from a perfect plane or sphere, measured in fractions of a wavelength (typically at 632.8nm HeNe). The flatter the surface, the more polishing and metrology time required, and the higher the cost.

λ/4 Flatness
Commercial / standard grade

Engineering Explanation

A λ/4 surface has a peak-to-valley wavefront error of up to one-quarter wavelength. In transmission, this means about λ/8 wavefront distortion (because light passes through the surface twice — once in, once out, but the surface figure error is divided by ~n-1).

Real Impact

Sufficient for general imaging, illumination, and low-power laser systems. The wavefront error is well within the depth of focus of most f/4 and slower lenses. Barely detectable in typical visual systems.

Cost Impact

Baseline cost. Standard polished surface with minimal extra metrology. Most economical choice for non-critical applications.

λ/10 Flatness
Precision grade

Engineering Explanation

λ/10 flatness means the surface deviates by no more than 0.1 waves peak-to-valley. This requires careful polishing with frequent interferometric feedback and careful process control.

Real Impact

Necessary for metrology systems, interferometry, high-resolution imaging, and moderate-power laser resonators. Delivers diffraction-limited performance for most optical systems. λ/10 is the sweet spot for many precision applications.

Cost Impact

Roughly 1.5–2× the cost of λ/4. Extra polishing time and more frequent metrology add cost, but the performance gain is significant for precision systems.

λ/20 Flatness
High-precision / laser grade

Engineering Explanation

λ/20 flatness represents 0.05 waves peak-to-valley error. Achieving this requires expert polishing, extended process time, and multiple interferometer measurements — often with sub-aperture polishing for final figure correction.

Real Impact

Required for high-precision interferometer reference flats, high-power laser resonators, ring laser gyroscopes, and systems where wavefront error must be at the absolute minimum. Often over-specified for general imaging or illumination.

Cost Impact

Typically 3–5× the cost of λ/4. Yield drops significantly at this level, and metrology time increases substantially. Only specify when the application genuinely demands it.

PhotonEdge Recommendation: Start with λ/4 for general purpose and λ/10 for precision systems. Only go to λ/20 if you can trace the flatness requirement to a specific system-level performance specification. Many systems get λ/20 performance from a λ/10 part because the spec has built-in margin that doesn't reflect real system needs.

Surface Quality: 10-5 vs 20-10 vs 40-20 (Scratch-Dig)

Surface quality describes the presence of cosmetic defects — scratches and digs — on the polished surface. It is specified per MIL-PRF-13830B or ISO 10110 standards using the scratch-dig code (e.g., 60-40, 40-20, 20-10, 10-5). The first number is the scratch grade, the second is the dig grade. Lower numbers = fewer/smaller defects = higher cost.

40-20
Commercial / standard grade

Engineering Explanation

40-20 surface quality allows scratches up to 0.04mm wide and digs up to 0.2mm in diameter (per MIL spec interpretation). Surfaces are polished and inspected under standard lighting conditions.

Real Impact

Perfectly acceptable for most imaging, illumination, and low-power laser applications. Scattered light from cosmetic defects is negligible in non-critical systems. The human eye cannot distinguish 40-20 from 10-5 in most finished optical systems.

Cost Impact

Baseline cost. Standard polishing with visual inspection. The most economical choice for the vast majority of general optical applications.

20-10
Precision grade

Engineering Explanation

20-10 surface quality allows scratches up to 0.02mm wide and digs up to 0.1mm diameter. Requires more careful polishing, clean handling, and inspection under dark-field illumination.

Real Impact

Recommended for high-power lasers (where defects can initiate damage), imaging systems with high dynamic range, and any application where scattered light could reduce signal-to-noise ratio. Good balance between quality and cost for precision systems.

Cost Impact

Approximately 1.3–1.8× the cost of 40-20. Extra polishing time, clean-room handling, and more thorough inspection add moderate cost. Often a worthwhile upgrade for laser and imaging systems.

10-5
High-precision / laser grade

Engineering Explanation

10-5 surface quality allows only very fine scratches (0.01mm max) and tiny digs (0.05mm max). Requires final polishing in a clean environment with meticulous inspection under high-intensity dark-field illumination.

Real Impact

Essential for high-energy pulsed lasers (where surface defects concentrate energy and cause laser damage), ultra-high-vacuum systems, and the most demanding low-scatter applications. Often over-specified for visible imaging systems.

Cost Impact

Typically 2–4× the cost of 40-20. Yield drops significantly — many parts must be re-polished or rejected. Clean-room handling and extended inspection drive cost up substantially.

Common Misconception: Many engineers specify 10-5 because they think it means "better quality" without considering whether cosmetic defects actually affect their system performance. For an imaging lens with 10 elements, the combined effect of 40-20 surfaces on image contrast is typically far less than other error sources (like lens design, alignment, or coating quality).
PhotonEdge Recommendation: Use 40-20 for general imaging and illumination, 20-10 for lasers above 100mW and precision systems, and 10-5 only for high-energy pulsed lasers or applications where scatter is genuinely critical. If you're not sure, ask: will a 0.02mm scratch on this surface actually impact my system's performance? If the answer is "probably not," don't pay for 10-5.

Dimensional Tolerance: ±0.01mm vs ±0.05mm vs ±0.1mm

Dimensional tolerances specify how precisely the physical size (diameter, thickness, edge thickness) of an optical component must match the nominal value. Tighter dimensional tolerance requires more careful grinding, more precise metrology, and more rejects.

±0.1mm
Commercial grade

Engineering Explanation

±0.1mm (or 100 micron) diameter/thickness tolerance is achievable with standard optical grinding and polishing processes. Final dimensions are verified with standard digital micrometers.

Real Impact

Sufficient for most lens cells and mounts with retaining rings, where the optic is centered by a bevel or spring. Thickness variation of 0.1mm has negligible optical effect in most systems (focal length shift is typically far less than 1%).

Cost Impact

Baseline cost. Standard optical manufacturing tolerances. Most catalog components fall into this range because it provides excellent value for general applications.

±0.05mm
Precision grade

Engineering Explanation

±0.05mm (50 micron) tolerance requires more controlled grinding processes and careful measurement. May require centerless grinding for OD and precision lapping for thickness control.

Real Impact

Useful for precision lens barrels, drop-in assemblies, and applications where the optic must fit closely into a bore without adhesive or shimming. Ensures consistent fit in medium-volume assemblies.

Cost Impact

Approximately 1.2–1.5× the cost of ±0.1mm. Moderate additional processing and more frequent measurement. Generally worth specifying if your mechanical design requires close fit.

±0.01mm
High-precision grade

Engineering Explanation

±0.01mm (10 micron) tolerance is very tight for optical components. It requires precision grinding, possibly post-polishing centering, and metrology with gauge blocks or coordinate measuring machines.

Real Impact

Needed only for the most demanding optomechanical assemblies — interferometer reference mounts, precision rotary stages, and applications where the part must locate with micron-level repeatability. Rarely necessary for general optical systems.

Cost Impact

Typically 2–3× the cost of ±0.1mm. Additional centering operations, precision metrology, and lower yields all contribute to significantly higher cost.

Important: Dimensional tolerance and optical performance are not directly linked. A lens with ±0.1mm diameter tolerance focuses light just as well as one with ±0.01mm. The tolerance matters only for mechanical fit — how the part sits in its mount. If your mount uses a retaining ring or spring, the diameter tolerance rarely matters at all.
PhotonEdge Recommendation: Use ±0.1mm for most applications with standard mounts. Specify ±0.05mm if the optic must drop into a precision bore without shimming. Only go to ±0.01mm if you have a specific mechanical reason (e.g., interchangeable optics in a precision mount). Don't confuse mechanical precision with optical performance.

The Core Principle

Tighter tolerances mean higher cost — but not always better system performance.

The goal of specification optimization is to identify which parameters genuinely affect your system's performance and allocate your budget there, while relaxing the parameters that don't matter.
