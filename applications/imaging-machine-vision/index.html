Imaging & Machine Vision Optics

High-quality imaging optics for industrial inspection systems that demand resolution, low distortion, and batch-to-batch consistency on high-speed production lines.

1. What Optical Problem Does This Industry Have?

Industrial inspection and machine vision systems rely on high-quality imaging optics to perform precise measurement and defect detection on high-speed production lines. The core optical challenges include:

  • Resolution — resolving fine features at the required working distance, often down to micrometer-level pixel size
  • Distortion control — keeping geometric distortion below 0.1% for accurate metrology and gauging applications
  • Environmental adaptability — stable performance across temperature variations, vibration, and particulate-contaminated factory floors
  • Batch consistency — every unit must meet the same optical specification; component-level variation directly impacts system calibration

2. What Specifications Are Critical?

Resolution (MTF)

Modulation Transfer Function at specified spatial frequencies; determines the system's ability to resolve fine features.

Distortion < 0.1%

Geometric distortion must be minimal for metrology and precision gauging applications.

Working Distance & DOF

Clear aperture and depth of field must accommodate mechanical tolerances and object height variation.

Wavelength Range

Visible (400-700 nm) for color inspection; NIR (700-1000 nm) for silicon-based sensors; UV for high-resolution inspection.

Environmental Sealing

IP65/IP67 protection for optics exposed to dust, coolant mist, or washdown environments.

Telecentricity

Object-space telecentric lenses eliminate perspective error, critical for dimensional measurement.

3. What Materials Are Commonly Used?

BK7 Optical Glass

350 nm – 2 μm

The standard lens material for visible-range machine vision systems. Good optical homogeneity, cost-effective for volume production.

Optical Glass Windows

BK7 / B270 / D263

Protective windows for camera enclosures and sensor modules. Flatness and parallelism control is critical to avoid wavefront distortion.

Sapphire Protection Windows

150 nm – 5.5 μm

For harsh industrial environments where scratch resistance and chemical durability are required. Mohs hardness 9.

Recommended Coatings

Coating selection depends on wavelength, power level and application environment. Common options for this application:

AR Coatings (Visible)

Anti-reflection coatings for visible range (400-700 nm). Reduces ghosting and flare in imaging lenses, increases light throughput by 3-4% per surface.

BBAR (Broadband AR)

Broadband AR coatings across VIS-NIR (400-1000 nm) for systems using silicon-based sensors. Covers both color inspection and near-infrared applications.

Metallic Coatings

Protected silver and aluminum coatings for illumination mirrors and beam steering optics in machine vision systems.

Hard Protective Coatings

Abrasion-resistant overcoats for production environment optics. Protects against cleaning agents, mechanical wiping and particulate contamination on the factory floor.

Hard Coat (Abrasion Resistant)

Protective overcoat for optics in contact with cleaning agents or mechanical wiping. Extends service life in production environments.

Low-surface-energy coating repels fingerprints, oils, and coolant residue. Reduces maintenance downtime for lens cleaning.

5. What Manufacturing Challenges Exist?

Batch Consistency

Machine vision systems are calibrated once and deployed across hundreds of inspection stations. Every replacement optic must perform identically — requiring tight process control and statistical quality management.

Low Distortion Precision

Achieving <0.1% distortion requires precise control of lens element centering, surface form accuracy, and assembly alignment.

Environmental Stability

Optical performance must remain stable across operating temperature ranges (-20°C to +60°C typical). Thermal focus shift and coating adhesion under thermal cycling are critical concerns.
