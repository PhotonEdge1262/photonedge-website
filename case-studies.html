Case Study #1 · Laser Systems

UV Laser Window Optimization for 355nm Industrial System

UV Laser
Problem
Standard BK7 windows caused laser damage and thermal lensing at 355nm, 10W average power
Result
Fused silica windows with optimized UV AR coating — zero damage after 2000+ hours at 15W

Customer Problem

A manufacturer of industrial UV laser marking systems was experiencing premature laser-induced damage on the output protective window of their 355nm, 10W DPSS laser. Standard BK7 windows from their existing supplier showed haze formation after 200-300 hours of operation, reducing output power by 15-20%.

System Requirement

Output protective window for 355nm DPSS laser, 10W average power, 25mm diameter, > 95% transmission, 10,000-hour operational lifetime with < 5% power degradation.

Root Cause

BK7 glass absorbs significantly at 355nm (~10-15% per cm), causing thermal lensing and coating degradation. The standard VIS AR coating was not optimized for UV wavelengths and had higher absorption at 355nm, accelerating damage.

Engineering Analysis

At 355nm and 10W average power with ~1mm beam diameter, intensity is ~127 W/cm². BK7's absorption coefficient at 355nm is ~0.1 cm⁻¹, meaning ~1% absorption per mm of material. In a 3mm window, that's ~3% absorption, depositing ~300mW of heat — enough for thermal lensing and coating degradation.

Material Selection

UV-grade fused silica was selected for its exceptional transmission at 355nm (> 99.5% per cm), low thermal expansion coefficient, and high UV laser damage threshold. Fused silica absorbs < 0.01% per cm at 355nm — 100× less than BK7.

Optical Specification

25.4mm diameter, 3mm thick UV fused silica window. 20-10 surface quality, λ/10 flatness. 30-arc-minute wedge to prevent etalon effects. UV AR coating on both sides optimized for 355nm, R < 0.5% per surface.

Manufacturing Process

Blank preparation from UVFS boule → double-sided grinding → fine grinding → precision polishing to λ/10 → edge chamfering → wedge control → UV AR coating deposition (ion-beam sputtering) → final inspection.

Inspection

Interferometer flatness test (verified λ/10 per surface), spectral transmission measurement at 355nm (measured T > 99%), surface quality dark-field inspection (confirmed 20-10), wedge angle measurement (29.4 arc min nominal), LIDT sample test (3 J/cm² @ 355nm, 10ns).

Business Impact

Customer eliminated a major field failure mode, reduced warranty costs from window replacements by approximately 60%, and improved the MTBF of their laser systems from ~500 hours to over 5000 hours. The 35nm fused silica windows now ship as standard in all their UV laser models.

Lessons Learned

Material selection is critical at UV wavelengths where even small absorption coefficients lead to significant thermal loading at moderate power levels. BK7 is not an appropriate substrate for 355nm applications above ~1W average power. Coating quality and deposition method (IBS vs. E-beam) also significantly impact UV LIDT.

Case Study #2 · Medical Imaging

Precision Imaging Lens Assembly for Diagnostic Instrument

Medical Imaging
Problem
Imported lens assemblies from US supplier were 4× too expensive and had 12-week lead times for production volumes
Result
Custom achromatic triplet assembly at 60% lower cost, meeting all MTF and distortion specs, 4-week production lead time

Customer Problem

A medical diagnostics company was sourcing a custom 3-element achromatic lens assembly from a US-based optics manufacturer for their in-vitro diagnostic instrument. Cost per unit was too high for volume production, and lead times were inconsistent, causing production scheduling problems.

System Requirement

3-element achromatic lens assembly, 25mm effective focal length, f/2.8, diffraction-limited MTF across 400-700nm, < 0.5% distortion, aligned in a threaded aluminum barrel, 500 units/year volume.

Root Cause

The US supplier was pricing for low-volume custom manufacturing with significant engineering overhead. The design, while optically excellent, used premium glass types and tight tolerances that drove cost without being strictly necessary for the application's actual performance needs.

Engineering Analysis

Our optical engineers reviewed the design and found that the specified λ/20 surface figure and 10-5 surface quality were significantly tighter than needed for a f/2.8 visible imaging system. Tolerance analysis showed that λ/10 flatness and 20-10 surface quality would deliver MTF within 1% of the tight-spec design.

Material Selection

Standard N-BK7 crown glass and SF11 flint glass for the achromatic elements. A third meniscus element in N-BK7 for field flattening. Verified all materials are available in production quantities with short lead times from multiple qualified suppliers.

Optical Specification

3-element lens assembly: 2 cemented doublets + 1 singlet. 25mm EFL, f/2.8. Surface quality: 20-10. Surface figure: λ/10. Centration: < 1 arc min. VIS broadband AR coating (400-700nm). All elements aligned in anodized aluminum barrel.

Manufacturing Process

Blank generation → curve generation → fine grinding → polishing → centering → cementing (doublets) → coating → barrel assembly → alignment → final MTF testing. Production set up for 500-unit annual volume with inventory buffers for key materials.

Inspection

100% MTF testing at 40 lp/mm and 80 lp/mm (sagittal and tangential). Distortion measurement via calibrated test target. Focal length verification. Surface quality inspection. Coating spectral verification. Environmental stress test (thermal cycling) on sampling basis.

Business Impact

Customer reduced lens assembly cost by 60% per unit, cutting annual procurement spend by approximately $120,000. Production lead time improved from 12 weeks to 4 weeks, enabling just-in-time inventory and reducing working capital tied up in component stock.

Lessons Learned

Tightening every specification "just to be safe" adds significant cost without proportional performance gain. Specification optimization — reducing tolerances that don't affect system performance while maintaining those that do — can deliver 30-60% cost savings on custom optics. The key is knowing which specs matter.

Case Study #3 · Semiconductor

High-Purity Fused Silica Windows for Wafer Inspection System

Semiconductor
Problem
Off-the-shelf fused silica windows had too much wavefront distortion and contamination, reducing inspection tool resolution
Result
Custom λ/20 fused silica windows with class 100 cleanroom packaging — achieved 20% higher resolution than target spec

Customer Problem

A semiconductor equipment manufacturer was building a next-generation wafer inspection tool and needed high-quality optical viewports for the imaging system. Standard catalog fused silica windows were causing wavefront error that limited the system's achievable resolution below their target.

System Requirement

100mm diameter × 10mm thick fused silica viewport window. Wavefront distortion < λ/10 transmitted. Surface quality 10-5. UV-enhanced AR coating at 365nm. Ultra-clean packaging suitable for class 100 cleanroom installation.

Root Cause

Standard catalog λ/4 windows introduce approximately λ/8 transmitted wavefront error per surface (doubled when counting both surfaces in a window), which is well above what a high-NA inspection objective can tolerate without degrading image resolution.

Engineering Analysis

For a 0.8 NA inspection system at 365nm, the diffraction-limited wavefront error budget is approximately λ/14. A standard λ/4 window would consume most of that budget, leaving no margin for the objective lens and other system elements. λ/20 surface flatness on both sides is required to keep window contribution below λ/10 total.

Material Selection

UV-grade fused silica (Corning 7980 equivalent) selected for excellent UV transmission at 365nm, low thermal expansion, and high homogeneity grade (H2). High-purity material with low OH content for minimal absorption and fluorescence.

Optical Specification

100.0mm diameter × 10.0mm thick. ±0.05mm diameter tolerance. ±0.02mm thickness tolerance. Both sides polished to λ/20 flatness. 10-5 surface quality per MIL-PRF-13830B. UV AR coating (350-400nm), R < 0.5% per surface.

Manufacturing Process

Core drill from fused silica blank → OD grinding → double-sided lapping → fine grinding both sides → precision polishing with iterative interferometer feedback to achieve λ/20 → IBS UV AR coating deposition → class 100 cleanroom cleaning → double packaging in cleanroom environment.

Inspection

Phase-shift interferometer on both surfaces (verified λ/25 and λ/22 respectively — both better than spec). Transmitted wavefront test: λ/12 PV. Surface quality inspection with 100× dark-field microscope (confirmed 10-5). UV transmission measured at 365nm: 99.3% both surfaces combined.

Business Impact

The customer's inspection tool achieved a 20% resolution improvement over their target specification, enabling them to detect smaller defect features than originally planned. This directly translated into a competitive advantage in their product positioning and opened up new market segments their previous tool couldn't address.

Lessons Learned

For high-NA imaging systems, window wavefront error must be carefully budgeted alongside all other system components. The flatness specification of a window in transmission has roughly twice the optical effect of its surface figure (light enters and exits, encountering both surfaces). Cleanroom packaging is essential for high-quality optics that will be installed in cleanroom equipment.

Case Study #4 · Ultrafast Lasers

Custom CaF₂ Prism Pair for Ultrafast Pulse Compression

Ultrafast Laser
Problem
Imported CaF₂ prism pairs had poor angle accuracy and inconsistent dispersion, causing unstable pulse compression in a femtosecond amplifier
Result
Matched CaF₂ prism pairs with < 5 arcsec angle tolerance and matched dispersion — pulse duration stable within 3% over 1000 hours

Customer Problem

A manufacturer of femtosecond CPA laser systems was experiencing pulse duration instability when using CaF₂ prism pairs from their existing supplier. Pairs from different batches produced different amounts of dispersion, requiring manual recalibration and limiting the unit-to-unit consistency of their laser product.

System Requirement

CaF₂ right-angle prism pair for pulse compression in a 1kHz, 1mJ, 800nm Ti:Sapphire amplifier. 25mm base length, 15mm height. Apex angle tolerance < 10 arc sec. Prism-to-prism GVD matching within 2%. Uncoated.

Root Cause

The previous supplier was cutting prisms from different parts of different CaF₂ boules without dispersion matching. Natural variation in CaF₂ refractive index (up to 0.0002 between boules) causes measurable GVD variation when accumulated through a prism pair with multiple passes.

Engineering Analysis

For a 4-pass prism compressor, the total GVD scales with both prism material dispersion and geometric path length. A 0.0002 refractive index variation between prisms in a pair creates approximately 2-3% GVD mismatch — enough to change pulse duration by 5-8 femtoseconds in a 50fs system, which was the customer's complaint.

Material Selection

High-purity single-crystal CaF₂, <111> orientation, from the same boule for matched prism pairs. UV-grade CaF₂ specified for 700-1000nm transmission. Material certified for low striae and homogeneous refractive index across the boule.

Optical Specification

Right-angle isosceles prism, 25mm leg length, 15mm clear aperture height. Apex angle: 90° ± 5 arc sec. Base angle: 45° ± 5 arc sec. Surface quality: 20-10. Surface figure: λ/10 per surface. Pyramid angle error < 10 arc sec. Uncoated (as-polished).

Manufacturing Process

Single CaF₂ boule cut into matched prism blanks (both prisms from adjacent slices) → rough grinding to prism shape → fine grinding → precision polishing all three faces → angle verification on precision goniometer → final cleaning → matched pair packaging with matched serial numbers.

Inspection

Apex angle measured on precision goniometer (3.2 arc sec and 4.1 arc sec for pair #1 — both within spec). Refractive index verified via minimum deviation method for both prisms (matched to < 0.00005 difference). Surface quality inspected (20-10 confirmed). Flatness verified via interferometer.

Business Impact

Customer eliminated the need for manual pulse duration calibration between production units, reducing assembly time by approximately 4 hours per laser. Unit-to-unit consistency improved from 15% pulse duration variation to < 3%, enabling them to ship calibrated systems with tighter published specifications.

Lessons Learned

For precision applications like ultrafast pulse compression, material homogeneity between matched components can be as important as the dimensional tolerances. Specifying that both prisms come from the same boule (or at minimum the same boule grade) is critical for consistent GVD performance. Angle accuracy of a few arc seconds matters when light makes multiple passes through a prism pair.
