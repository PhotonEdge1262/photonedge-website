BK7 vs UV Fused Silica: When to Choose Which

A practical engineering comparison of the two most common optical materials — helping you avoid costly material selection mistakes in lens, window, and substrate design.

Why This Comparison Matters

BK7 (N-BK7, borosilicate crown glass) and UV Fused Silica are the two most frequently specified optical materials worldwide. Together they cover the majority of lens, window, prism, and substrate applications. Yet optical designers still over-specify fused silica for visible-light systems (paying 3–5× more than necessary) or mistakenly choose BK7 for UV applications (causing transmission failures at wavelengths below 350 nm).

This guide provides a direct, property-level comparison so you can make the right choice for your application — without over-engineering or under-performing.

When BK7 Is the Right Choice

N-BK7 is the default workhorse optical material. It should be your first choice unless specific application requirements rule it out. Choose BK7 when:

  • Visible to near-IR operation (400–1,800 nm): BK7 provides excellent transmission across the entire visible spectrum and well into the near-infrared. Most imaging, collimation, beam steering, and illumination optics operate in this range.
  • General-purpose lenses and prisms: Plano-convex lenses, achromat elements, Dove prisms, right-angle prisms — BK7 is the standard material for catalog optics.
  • Cost-sensitive designs: BK7 blanks are widely available, easy to fabricate, and well-understood by all optical shops. Lead times are short and scrap rates are low.
  • Moderate thermal environments: For systems operating between −20°C and +60°C with no extreme thermal cycling, BK7’s expansion is manageable with standard mechanical design.
  • Low to moderate power lasers: For alignment lasers, pointer-class, or diagnostic beams below ~1 W/cm², BK7 substrates with appropriate coatings are acceptable.
  • Prototyping and R&D: When you need fast turnaround and iteration, BK7’s availability and lower cost make it the practical choice.
Engineering Tip If your system operates entirely above 400 nm and below 1,800 nm, has no high-power laser exposure, and does not require athermal performance, BK7 is almost certainly the right material. Save fused silica for where you actually need it.

When Fused Silica Is Necessary

UV Fused Silica becomes necessary (not just preferable) when one or more of these conditions exist:

  • Deep UV transmission required (<350 nm): Applications such as fluorescence microscopy (DAPI/Hoechst at 340–360 nm), lithography optics (i-line at 365 nm), UV curing systems, and spectroscopy below 350 nm all demand fused silica. BK7 absorption below 350 nm makes it unusable.
  • High-power laser systems: For CW lasers above ~10 W or pulsed lasers with peak intensities above ~10 J/cm² (nanosecond regime), fused silica’s superior laser damage threshold (LIDT) is essential. BK7 substrates can develop thermal lensing or catastrophic damage.
  • Precision athermal designs: Interferometers, reference cavities, space-borne optics, and systems requiring wavefront stability over wide temperature ranges benefit from fused silica’s ~13× lower thermal expansion coefficient.
  • UV fluorescence and Raman spectroscopy: Windows and collection optics for UV-excited fluorescence must transmit both the excitation and emission wavelengths. Fused silica covers 185–2,500 nm in a single material.
  • Excimer and exciplex laser systems: ArF (193 nm), KrF (248 nm), and XeCl (308 nm) lasers require fused silica optics rated for deep-UV operation with appropriate coatings.
  • Low-birefringence requirements: Polarization-sensitive systems (ellipsometry, precision polarimetry) benefit from fused silica’s extremely low intrinsic stress birefringence.
Common Mistake: Over-specifying Fused Silica Many engineers specify fused silica “just to be safe” for visible-light imaging systems. This adds 3–5× material cost, longer lead times for large blanks, and can actually degrade achromatic correction since fused silica’s dispersion properties differ from BK7. Only specify FS when a real requirement exists.

Material Selection Decision Guide

Use this step-by-step framework to select between BK7 and fused silica:

  1. What is your operating wavelength?
    • Below 350 nm → Fused Silica (mandatory)
    • 350–2,000 nm → Both work; continue to step 2
    • Above 2,000 nm → Neither is ideal; consider ZnSe, Si, or Ge
  2. Is high laser power involved?
    • CW >10 W or pulsed >10 J/cm² → Fused Silica (LIDT advantage)
    • Lower power → BK7 acceptable; continue to step 3
  3. Does thermal stability matter?
    • Operating range >80°C or wavefront stability <λ/4 over temperature → Fused Silica
    • Room-temperature or controlled environment → BK7 is fine
  4. Is polarization purity critical?
    • Yes (ellipsometry, precision polarimetry) → Fused Silica
    • No → BK7 acceptable
  5. Is budget a primary constraint?
    • Yes and no other driver above applies → BK7

Common Material Selection Mistakes

Mistake 1: Using BK7 for UV Applications

BK7 contains iron impurities that cause strong absorption below 350 nm. At 300 nm, BK7 transmission is essentially zero for any meaningful thickness. Designers new to UV systems sometimes specify BK7 because it is the “default” optical glass, then discover their optics are opaque at the operating wavelength. Solution: Always check the transmission curve. For any wavelength below 350 nm, specify fused silica or CaF₂.

Mistake 2: Over-specifying Fused Silica for Visible Systems

Some engineers specify fused silica for all optics “because it’s better.” But in visible imaging systems, fused silica’s lower refractive index (1.458 vs 1.517) means you need steeper curvatures to achieve the same optical power, potentially increasing aberrations. It also disrupts achromatic doublet designs optimized for BK7/SF-glass pairs. Solution: Use fused silica only when UV, laser power, or thermal stability demand it.

Mistake 3: Ignoring dn/dT in Thermal Analysis

While fused silica has much lower CTE than BK7, its dn/dT (9.6 × 10−6/K) is actually higher than BK7’s (3.0 × 10−6/K). The total thermal optical power shift depends on both CTE and dn/dT. In some athermal designs, BK7 can actually outperform fused silica when both effects are considered together. Solution: Calculate the total thermo-optic coefficient, not just CTE.

Mistake 4: Forgetting Coating Compatibility

AR coatings designed for BK7 (n=1.517) will not perform optimally on fused silica (n=1.458), and vice versa. The layer thicknesses in multi-layer coatings are calculated for specific substrate indices. Always specify the substrate material when ordering coated optics. Solution: See our AR Coating Selection Guide for details on matching coatings to substrate materials.

Cost vs Performance Tradeoffs

Understanding the cost structure helps you make rational tradeoffs:

Factor BK7 UV Fused Silica
Raw blank cost (Ø25 mm) Low (baseline) 3–5× BK7
Raw blank cost (Ø100 mm) Moderate 4–8× BK7
Polishing difficulty Standard Slightly harder (higher softening point)
Typical lead time (standard catalog) 1–2 weeks 2–4 weeks
Coating cost Standard Similar (may need UV-rated coating materials)
Cost of wrong material choice System failure in UV 3–5× unnecessary cost

The real cost question is not “which material is cheaper” but “what is the cost of getting the material wrong?” Choosing BK7 for a UV system means complete optical failure — no amount of cost savings compensates. Conversely, specifying fused silica for a simple visible LED collimator means paying 3–5× more for no performance benefit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use BK7 for 365 nm UV-A applications?

BK7 has marginal transmission at 365 nm — typically 60–70% per 10 mm thickness depending on the specific melt. For demanding UV-A applications, fused silica is strongly recommended. If you must use BK7 near 365 nm, request a transmission curve from the manufacturer for your specific batch and keep thickness minimal.

Is fused silica really worth 3–5× the cost of BK7?

Only if your application requires its specific advantages: UV transmission, high LIDT, or thermal stability. For standard visible-light imaging, collimation, or beam steering, BK7 provides equivalent performance at a fraction of the cost. The cost premium is justified for laser systems, UV instruments, and precision references.

What about UV-grade BK7 — is that a compromise?

UV-grade BK7 (with tighter iron content control) improves transmission near 350 nm but does not reach true deep-UV performance. It can be a reasonable choice for 340–365 nm applications where fused silica cost is prohibitive, but it is not a substitute for fused silica below 330 nm.

Can I mix BK7 and fused silica elements in the same lens design?

Yes, but be aware that their different refractive indices (1.517 vs 1.458) and dispersion properties (Vd 64.2 vs 67.8) will affect the optical design. Achromatic doublets are typically designed with BK7 paired with high-index flint glasses, not fused silica. Hybrid designs are possible but require optimization from the start.

Does fused silica’s higher dn/dT make it worse for some thermal applications?

In a word: sometimes. The total thermal shift of optical power depends on both CTE (which favors fused silica) and dn/dT (which favors BK7). For systems where the optical path length through air dominates (long focal lengths), BK7’s lower dn/dT can actually give better thermal stability. Always calculate the full thermo-optic effect for your specific design.
