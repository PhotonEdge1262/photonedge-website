1. Why Material Selection Matters

The optical material you choose determines virtually every aspect of your system's performance: what wavelengths can pass through, how the optic behaves under thermal stress, what laser power it can handle, how it survives in harsh environments, and ultimately how much it costs.

A wrong material choice can manifest as: unexpected UV absorption, thermal lensing in high-power lasers, birefringence artifacts in polarized systems, window fracture under thermal shock, or simply excessive cost for a performance level you don't need. Getting the material right from the start avoids costly redesigns and delays.

4. N-BK7: The Workhorse Optical Glass

N-BK7 is a borosilicate crown glass and the most widely used optical material worldwide. If your application operates in the visible to near-IR range (350-2000nm) with no extreme requirements, BK7 is almost certainly the right choice.

Advantages

  • Excellent optical homogeneity (Δn < 1 × 10-6)
  • Low cost and widely available in all standard forms
  • Good transmission from 350nm to 2μm
  • Well-characterized, decades of manufacturing data
  • Easy to polish to high surface quality

Limitations

  • Poor UV transmission below 350nm (iron absorption)
  • Moderate thermal expansion — not for precision over wide temperature ranges
  • Low laser damage threshold compared to Fused Silica
  • Not suitable for IR beyond 2μm

Typical Applications

Imaging lenses, beam steering, laboratory optics, educational setups, machine vision, general-purpose windows and prisms.

5. UV Fused Silica: Versatile from Deep UV to Near-IR

UV Fused Silica (Synthetic Fused Silica) is amorphous SiO₂ produced from high-purity silicon compounds. It is the go-to material when BK7 falls short — especially for UV transmission, high-power lasers, and thermally stable applications.

Key Advantages Over BK7

  • Deep UV transmission down to 180nm (vs 350nm for BK7)
  • ~13x lower CTE — minimal thermal expansion
  • 2-5x higher laser damage threshold
  • Lower dn/dT — reduced thermal lensing
  • Higher homogeneity available (CVI, Heraeus grades)

When NOT to Use Fused Silica

  • When cost is the primary driver (2-3x more expensive than BK7)
  • When high refractive index is needed (n=1.458 vs BK7's 1.517)
  • When you need low dispersion (Abbe numbers are similar, but BK7 is slightly better)

6. Sapphire (Al₂O₃): Extreme Durability

Sapphire is the second hardest natural material (9 Mohs), making it ideal for optics exposed to harsh environments, abrasion, or high-pressure cleaning. It transmits from 150nm to 5.5μm — covering UV through mid-IR.

Best Applications

  • Protective windows in industrial environments
  • Medical device windows requiring autoclave sterilization
  • Scratch-resistant display covers and viewports
  • High-pressure sensor windows
  • Military and aerospace optics
Important: Sapphire is Birefringent Sapphire is a uniaxial crystal with Δn ≈ 0.008. This birefringence causes polarization-dependent effects that can be problematic in polarization-sensitive systems. For non-polarizing applications, ensure the optic axis is oriented appropriately or use random-polycrystalline alternatives.

7. ZnSe: The CO2 Laser Standard

Zinc Selenide (ZnSe) is the dominant material for CO2 laser systems (10.6μm) and thermal imaging. It offers excellent transmission from 0.5μm to 20μm with relatively low absorption at CO2 wavelengths.

Key Properties

  • Transmission: 0.5-20μm (broadest useful range of any IR material)
  • Low absorption at 10.6μm (~0.0005/cm for premium grade)
  • Good thermal shock resistance (moderate CTE, reasonable conductivity)
  • Available with AR coatings optimized for CO2 laser wavelength

Handling Precautions

  • Relatively soft (Knoop 120) — scratch easily
  • Slightly toxic — avoid generating dust during processing
  • susceptible to thermal shock from rapid cooling

8. Germanium: LWIR Thermal Imaging

Germanium has the highest refractive index of any common optical material (n≈4.0 at 10μm), enabling compact lens designs with strong optical power from nearly-flat surfaces. It is the standard material for LWIR (8-14μm) thermal imaging systems.

Advantages

  • Highest refractive index — enables compact, fast F/# designs
  • Excellent transmission in LWIR band
  • High hardness (780 Knoop) — very durable for IR material
  • High thermal conductivity (60 W/m·K) — good for thermal environments

Limitations

  • Opaque below 2μm — completely unusable for visible applications
  • dn/dT is very high (60 × 10-6/K) — significant thermal defocus
  • Expensive raw material
  • For athermal designs, must be combined with chalcogenide or ZnSe elements

9. Silicon: Cost-Effective MWIR

Silicon is widely used for MWIR (3-5μm) optics, offering a cost-effective alternative to Germanium in the mid-IR. It has lower density than Ge (2.33 vs 5.33 g/cm³), making it preferred for weight-sensitive applications.

Best For

  • MWIR (3-5μm) imaging and sensing systems
  • Weight-sensitive applications (aerospace, drones)
  • Silicon is compatible with semiconductor manufacturing processes
  • Low-cost IR optics when diamond-turned surface is acceptable

10. CaF₂: Broad Spectral Range, Low Dispersion

Calcium Fluoride (CaF₂) is a crystalline material with exceptional transmission from deep UV (130nm) through mid-IR (7μm). Its very low dispersion (Abbe number 95) makes it irreplaceable for apochromatic lens designs and UV laser systems.

Key Applications

  • Excimer laser optics (193nm ArF, 248nm KrF)
  • APOCHROMATIC lens elements (paired with BK7 or other glasses)
  • Infrared spectroscopy cells and windows
  • Satellite and space-based optical systems (good radiation resistance)
Handling Note: CaF₂ is relatively soft and has a pronounced cleavage plane. It requires careful handling and specialized mounting to avoid stress fractures. Thermal shock resistance is moderate — avoid rapid temperature changes.

11. MgF₂: Deep UV Durability

Magnesium Fluoride (MgF₂) transmits from 115nm (deepest UV of any common optical material) to 7μm. It is harder and more durable than CaF₂, though with slightly narrower overall spectral range.

Unique Advantages

  • Deepest UV transmission of any practical window/lens material (VUV capable)
  • Higher hardness than CaF₂ — more resistant to scratching
  • Uniaxial crystal, birefringent (can be useful for UV polarizers)
  • Excellent as single-layer AR coating material on many substrates

12. Material Selection Decision Framework

Use this decision framework to narrow down your material choice:

Step 1: Determine Your Wavelength Range

  • Deep UV (<200nm): MgF₂ or CaF₂
  • UV (200-350nm): UV Fused Silica or CaF₂
  • Visible (400-700nm): N-BK7 (default), Fused Silica, or Sapphire
  • NIR (700-2500nm): BK7, Fused Silica, or Silicon (for >1.2μm)
  • SWIR (2-5μm): Silicon, ZnSe, CaF₂, or Sapphire
  • MWIR (3-5μm): Germanium, ZnSe, or Silicon
  • LWIR (8-14μm): Germanium or ZnSe
  • Far IR (>14μm): ZnSe, polyethylene, or specialized IR materials

Step 2: Check Environmental Requirements

  • High abrasion/scratch risk → Sapphire
  • High temperature / thermal cycling → Fused Silica (low CTE) or Silicon (high conductivity)
  • High humidity / chemical exposure → Fused Silica or Sapphire (avoid CaF₂, ZnSe)
  • Vacuum / space environment → Fused Silica, CaF₂ (check outgassing)

Step 3: Check Laser Requirements

  • High-power CW laser → Fused Silica (VIS-NIR), Silicon/Cu (IR)
  • High-power pulsed laser → Fused Silica with IBS coatings
  • UV laser (excimer) → CaF₂ or Fused Silica
  • CO2 laser (10.6μm) → ZnSe

Step 4: Cost Optimization

Within your viable material set, choose the most economical option that meets all requirements. BK7 is almost always cheapest for visible optics. For IR materials, Silicon is generally cheaper than Germanium. Fused Silica is 2-3x the cost of BK7 for visible applications.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common optical glass material?

N-BK7 (borosilicate crown glass) is the most widely used optical material for visible to near-IR applications. It offers excellent homogeneity, good transmission from 350nm to 2μm, and low cost. It is the default choice when no special requirements (UV, IR, high temperature, or high power) exist.

When should I use UV Fused Silica instead of BK7?

Choose UV Fused Silica when you need: (1) deep UV transmission below 350nm, (2) higher laser damage threshold, (3) lower thermal expansion for temperature-sensitive applications, or (4) better transmission uniformity in the UV. Fused Silica transmits from 180nm to 2.1μm and has 5x lower CTE than BK7.

What is the best material for CO2 laser optics at 10.6μm?

ZnSe (Zinc Selenide) is the standard choice for CO2 laser systems. It offers >67% transmission at 10.6μm with excellent thermal conductivity. For lower-power applications (<50W), single-crystal Silicon provides a cost-effective alternative. Germanium works but has higher absorption at 10.6μm than ZnSe.

How do I choose between Sapphire and Fused Silica for windows?

Sapphire excels when you need extreme hardness (9 on Mohs scale), superior scratch resistance, and good transmission from 150nm to 5.5μm. It has higher thermal conductivity than Fused Silica. Choose Fused Silica when you need better UV transmission, lower birefringence (sapphire is birefringent), or lower cost for large optics.

What is the best infrared optical material for thermal imaging?

For MWIR (3-5μm) thermal imaging, Germanium is the standard with highest refractive index (n~4.0) enabling compact lens designs. For LWIR (8-14μm), Germanium or ZnS (multispectral grade) are typical choices. ZnSe works across both bands but is softer. Chalcogenide glasses offer moldable aspheric IR optics for volume production.

Can I use BK7 for UV applications below 350nm?

BK7 transmission drops significantly below 350nm due to iron impurity absorption. For UV applications below 350nm, use UV Fused Silica (down to 180nm), CaF₂ (down to 130nm), or MgF₂ (down to 115nm). If you must use BK7 near 350nm, specify UV-grade BK7 with tighter iron content limits.

What optical material has the lowest thermal expansion?

Among common optical materials, ULE (Ultra-Low Expansion) glass and Zerodur have near-zero CTE (<0.05 × 10₊/K). UV Fused Silica has very low CTE (5.5 × 10₊/K). For most practical applications where extreme stability isn't needed, Fused Silica provides excellent thermal performance at much lower cost than ULE or Zerodur.

What material should I use for high-power laser optics?

For high-power CW lasers: UV Fused Silica (for VIS-NIR) or Silicon/Cu (for IR). For high-power pulsed lasers: UV Fused Silica with IBS coatings for VIS-NIR, CaF₂ for UV and deep-UV. The key factors are low absorption coefficient and high LIDT. Always specify IBS-deposited coatings for high-power applications.
